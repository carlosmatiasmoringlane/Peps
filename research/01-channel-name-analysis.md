# What Top Educational Channel Names Actually Have In Common

Research pass: September 2026. Sources listed at the bottom.

## The landscape I looked at

Two different "top educational channel" lists exist and they measure
different things, which is worth knowing before you copy anyone:

**The household names** (big, global, general-interest):
National Geographic (~23.6M), Kurzgesagt (~25M+), TED-Ed (~20.4M),
Veritasium (~14M), Khan Academy (~8.6M), plus CrashCourse, Numberphile,
AsapScience, Vsauce, MinutePhysics.

**The current algorithmic risers** (what ranking tools surface as the top
"Education"-category channels right now): ENGLISH with James / engVid
(~4.8M), NCERT Wallah (~2.9M), Las pequeñas (~2.7M),
The Armchair Historian (~2.5M).

The gap between those two lists is the single most useful finding here.
The risers are **narrow, named-audience channels** (one exam board, one
language, one era). The household names are **broad, coined-word brands**.
Both work. They are different businesses, and the name is where you commit
to one.

## The five naming patterns

### 1. Coined word — invent something that doesn't exist
`Veritasium` (Latin *veritas* + the `-ium` element suffix — "the element
of truth"), `Vsauce`, `Kurzgesagt` (German, "in short").

- **Why it wins:** zero search competition, instantly trademarkable, and
  the topic can drift anywhere without the name going stale.
- **Why it hurts:** the name teaches the viewer nothing. You pay for that
  with a tagline and with slower early growth.
- **The tell:** Kurzgesagt bolts on `– In a Nutshell`. The coined word is
  the brand; the plain-English half does the explaining. Copy that split.

### 2. Domain + modifier — say the subject, add the angle
`MinutePhysics` (physics, fast), `Practical Engineering` (engineering you
can use), `Real Engineering`, `PBS Space Time`.

- **Why it wins:** the viewer knows what they get in one glance, and the
  subject word is doing search work for you from day one.
- **Why it hurts:** it is a fence. `MinutePhysics` cannot credibly run a
  40-minute history episode.
- Best modifier types: speed (Minute), rigor (Real, Practical), or depth.

### 3. Outcome or identity — name the change in the viewer
`SmarterEveryDay`, `CrashCourse`.

- **Why it wins:** sells a feeling, not a topic, so it survives any pivot.
  This is the most durable category and the hardest to execute.

### 4. Persona — a character presents the material
`The Armchair Historian`, `Physics Girl`, `LegalEagle` (rhyme + domain).

- **Why it wins:** parasocial pull; people subscribe to a person.
- **Why it hurts:** it does not sell, does not scale past you, and you can
  never take a month off.
- Note this is the pattern doing best in the *riser* list.

### 5. Suffix play — `-phile`, `-ology`, `-ium`
`Numberphile`. Signals obsessive depth to a niche audience. Reads slightly
dated in 2026; use sparingly.

## The rules that hold across all five

1. **One to three words.** Every channel above. No exceptions.
2. **Sayable out loud without spelling it.** The single biggest practical
   filter. If a viewer cannot repeat it to a friend, word-of-mouth dies.
   (`Kurzgesagt` is the exception that proves it — and it needed a
   ten-year head start plus a tagline to survive the handicap.)
3. **Unique enough to own page one of search.** Coined words win here.
4. **Leave room to grow.** Pick the name for the channel you want in three
   years, not the first ten videos.
5. **Handle parity.** Same string on YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and a
   domain. Check before you fall in love with one.
6. **Pair a coined name with a plain tagline.** Non-negotiable if you go
   with pattern 1.

## Recommendation

Run **pattern 1 + a pattern 2 tagline** — a coined brand word carrying a
plain-English descriptor. It buys the search advantage and the freedom to
change topics, while the tagline pays the comprehension cost up front.
