# Shorts Hook Tests — Episode 01

Five Shorts. Three are the Episode 01 hooks built out as standalone
videos; two probe future topics. The point is not to promote the episode —
it's to find out **which opening earns the animation budget** before you
spend it.

- **Format:** 9:16 vertical, 35–45 seconds (the ceiling that still holds
  retention *if the payoff is explicit* — see `../../research/02-what-gets-views.md`)
- **Cadence:** 2–3 per week, one hook family at a time
- **Rule:** each Short must fully pay off on its own. A Short that ends
  in "watch the full video" is an ad, and it will be treated like one.

---

## The structure every Short uses

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0:00–0:03 | One provocation. No logo, no greeting, no windup. |
| Turn | 0:03–0:12 | Why the obvious answer is wrong. |
| Body | 0:12–0:32 | The real mechanism, one idea at a time. |
| Payoff | 0:32–0:40 | Close the loop. Fully. |
| Tag | 0:40–0:45 | Channel line. Optional — cut it if retention sags. |

---

## SHORT 1 — Statistic *(hook A)*

**On screen:** container centred, still. One number.

> Loading a ship used to cost about six dollars a ton.
> Then it dropped to about sixteen cents.
>
> No new engine. No cheaper fuel.
>
> Here's what actually happened. Before the 1950s, ships were loaded by
> hand — sacks, barrels, crates, every one a different shape, every one
> fitted like a puzzle piece by a person.
>
> So ships sat at the dock. Sometimes longer than they spent at sea.
> The expensive part of shipping was never the ocean. It was the waiting.
>
> Then someone put the cargo in identical boxes. Every crane, every truck,
> every port on Earth now handles **one shape.**
>
> That's the whole trick. Six dollars to sixteen cents, because everybody
> agreed on a size.

**Testing:** the number is doing all the work. If this underperforms, the
statistic isn't surprising enough on its own and Hook A is dead.

## SHORT 2 — Bold claim *(hook B)*

**On screen:** empty frame, then the container drops in on "steel box."

> The most important machine of the last century has no moving parts.
> No engine. No power supply.
>
> It's a steel box — and the clever part isn't the box.
>
> Anyone could have built it in 1900. The metalwork is trivial.
>
> The hard part took twenty years of committee meetings, because every
> shipping company had already built *their* container, and whoever's
> size won wouldn't have to replace their fleet. Everyone else would.
>
> It was never an engineering problem. It was an argument about whose
> money got written off.
>
> They settled on two sizes. And that agreement — not the steel — is why
> you can afford things made on the other side of the planet.

**Testing:** "machine with no moving parts" is a riddle. High-ceiling,
high-variance. If it lands, lead the channel with riddle hooks.

---

## SHORT 3 — Direct address *(hook C)*

**On screen:** ordinary objects appearing one by one, then all sliding
into the same container outline.

> Look at anything near you that was made somewhere else.
>
> Almost all of it spent part of its life inside the same object. Exactly
> the same shape. Exactly the same size. Everywhere on Earth.
>
> Your phone. Your chair. The shoes by the door.
>
> That wasn't an accident, and it wasn't a law. It was twenty years of
> argument that ended with the world agreeing a box would be **this** big
> and not some other size.
>
> Because a crane in Rotterdam and a truck in Ohio have to handle the
> same thing without ever having spoken.
>
> Most of what makes your life cheap is like this. Boring agreements
> nobody told you about.

**Testing:** safest of the three — "look around you" reliably holds the
first three seconds. Expect solid retention, lower ceiling. Use as the
control.

## SHORT 4 — Topic probe: plug sockets

Not from Episode 01. Tests whether the *channel premise* travels.

> Every country agreed on the size of a shipping container.
>
> Nobody agreed on plug sockets.
>
> There are about fifteen incompatible types in use right now. Not
> because it's hard — because by the time anyone tried to standardise,
> every country had already wired millions of buildings.
>
> Replacing them meant rewiring a nation. Keeping them meant you buy an
> adapter forever.
>
> Everyone picked the adapter.
>
> That's the thing about standards. There's a window where agreeing is
> cheap — and once it closes, it never reopens.

**Testing:** if this outperforms Shorts 1–3, the audience is buying the
*premise*, not the shipping topic. That would mean leading with the
pattern and using objects as examples.

---

## SHORT 5 — Topic probe: which side of the road *(seeds Episode 02)*

> About a third of the world drives on the left. Two thirds on the right.
>
> Neither one is safer. Neither one is better. There is no engineering
> reason for either.
>
> And yet almost no country has ever switched — because switching means
> changing every road sign, every bus door, every intersection, and every
> driving habit in the country, overnight, at the same moment.
>
> Sweden actually did it. One morning in 1967, the entire nation stopped,
> moved to the other side, and started again.
>
> Most things aren't the way they are because they're right.
> They're that way because changing costs too much.

**Testing:** direct demand-test for Episode 02. Strong performance here
justifies the full episode; weak performance means reorder the slate.

## Measuring it

Run each Short for **7 full days** before judging. Compare on:

1. **3-second retention** — the hook's actual score. Everything else is
   downstream of this.
2. **Average view duration as % of length** — did the body hold?
3. **Views** — noisiest signal, most affected by luck. Weight it least.

**Decide like this:** the Short with the best 3-second retention gives you
the hook family for Episode 01's final cut. If two are within a few
points, take the one with better *completion*, since that predicts
long-form behaviour better than the hook score alone.

Don't test two variables at once. Same thumbnail treatment, same voice,
same posting time — or you learn nothing.
