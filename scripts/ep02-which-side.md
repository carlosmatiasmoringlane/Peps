# Plainsight — Episode 02
## "Why Does Half The World Drive On The Wrong Side?"

- **Runtime target:** 8:00
- **Format:** counterintuitive explainer
- **Original script.** Written for this channel.

> **Note on the channel's through-line.** Episode 01 was about the power
> of *agreeing* on a standard. This one is the mirror image: what happens
> when you agree too late to change. The lesson is **lock-in**, not
> agreement — deliberately a different idea, so the channel doesn't have
> one thesis it repeats forever.

## Hook — three versions to test

### Hook A — Bold claim *(recommended)*
**[0:00–0:12]**

> About a third of the world drives on the left. Two thirds on the right.
>
> Neither is safer. Neither is better. There is no engineering reason for
> either one.
>
> And almost no country has ever managed to switch.

### Hook B — Story
**[0:00–0:13]**

> At five in the morning on a Sunday in 1967, every car in Sweden came to
> a complete stop.
>
> For a few minutes, an entire country sat still in the road.
>
> Then they all moved to the other side, and started driving again.

### Hook C — Question
**[0:00–0:10]**

> Why do you drive on the side you drive on?
>
> Not "what's the law" — why is the law *that*? Somebody chose. And
> once they did, it became almost impossible to unchoose.

## Full script

### [0:12] — THE OBVIOUS ANSWER IS WRONG

> The tempting explanation is that one side is safer. It isn't.

> Countries that drive on the left and countries that drive on the right
> don't split into safe and dangerous. Road deaths track things like
> enforcement, road design, vehicle age, and how much people drive —
> not which side they're on.

> So this isn't a safety story. It's a story about **how a choice gets
> stuck.**

### [0:50] — WHERE THE SIDES CAME FROM

> Go back far enough and the road has no sides at all. You walk where
> there's room.

> The usual story for keeping left is about weapons: most people are
> right-handed, so you pass an oncoming stranger on your left, keeping
> your right hand — your sword arm — between you and them. It's a good
> story. Treat it as a plausible tradition rather than a documented
> cause, because the honest answer is that the early record is thin.

> What's much better documented is what happened once **cargo** got
> heavy.

> In places like France and North America, freight moved on big wagons
> pulled by several pairs of horses, with no driver's seat. The driver
> rode the rear-left horse, so his right hand was free for the whip.

> Now think about where that puts his eyes. Sitting on the left, the way
> to watch your wheel clear an oncoming wagon is to keep **right.**

> Different vehicle, different body position, different answer.

### [1:50] — TWO EMPIRES MADE IT PERMANENT

> Then two things turned local habit into continental law.

> **First, Napoleon.** As French control spread across Europe,
> right-hand traffic spread with it. The map of who drives right in
> Europe still looks a lot like the map of who he reached.

> **Second, Britain** — which had formalised keeping left at home, and
> then administered a very large empire. Wherever British road rules were
> installed, left-hand traffic went too. That's a large part of why so
> much of the world still drives on the left today.

> Note what neither of those is: a finding. Nobody tested both and picked
> a winner. Two powers exported their own habit, and the habit set.

### [2:50] — THE CAR LOCKS IT IN

> So far this is reversible. A habit is cheap to change.

> Then came the car — and the car has a **steering wheel**, which means
> the choice stops living in people's heads and starts living in metal.

> Mass production made it worse, in the useful sense. Once cars were
> built in enormous identical runs, every vehicle in a country was
> manufactured for one side of the road. Change sides and you haven't
> changed a rule. You've made every car in the nation wrong.

> And it keeps going. Bus doors open on one side. Toll booths sit on one
> side. Motorway exits peel off in one direction. Every intersection in
> the country is drawn for one answer.

> **This is lock-in.** Not one big obstacle — a million small ones, each
> individually trivial, all pointing the same way.

### [4:00] — THE COUNTRY THAT DID IT ANYWAY

> Which brings us to Sweden.

> Sweden drove on the left. Every one of its neighbours drove on the
> right, and Swedes kept crossing borders into oncoming traffic. Worse,
> most cars in Sweden were already left-hand-drive — built for the other
> side. The country was driving on the wrong side *in the wrong cars.*

> A referendum had gone heavily **against** switching. The government
> did it anyway.

> They called it **Dagen H** — H for *högertrafik*, right-hand traffic.
> **3 September 1967.**

> And this is the part worth sitting with: you cannot phase this in.
> There is no version where half the country switches first. The change
> has to happen **everywhere, at the same instant.**

> So the entire country stopped. Non-essential traffic was banned from
> the roads. At a set moment, every vehicle still out halted, moved
> carefully across to the right, and waited. Then traffic started again.

> It took years of preparation — signs built in advance and covered,
> intersections redrawn, buses rebuilt, a public information campaign
> that reached essentially everyone in the country.

### [5:30] — THE RESULT NOBODY EXPECTS

> Here's the twist. Immediately after the switch, crashes went **down.**

> Not because right-hand traffic is better. Because everyone was
> terrified. People drove slowly, watched everything, and assumed nothing.

> And then, over the following months, the numbers drifted back toward
> where they'd been before.

> Which tells you something slightly uncomfortable: a large share of
> ordinary road risk isn't the road. It's that we stop paying attention.

### [6:20] — THE ONES THAT WENT THE OTHER WAY

> A couple of countries have gone the other direction, and they show what
> the real constraint is.

> **Samoa**, in 2009, switched from right to left — so that its people
> could import cheap used cars from nearby left-driving countries instead
> of expensive ones from far away. The road didn't change. The **car
> market** did.

> **Myanmar** switched from left to right decades ago, but kept importing
> right-hand-drive cars. So a lot of drivers there sit on the kerb side
> of the car, with the worst possible view of oncoming traffic, and
> overtaking is genuinely dangerous.

> That's what happens when you change the rule and the metal doesn't
> follow.

### [7:00] — THE TURN

> So: which side should you drive on?

> It doesn't matter. It has never mattered. Both work perfectly, as long
> as **everyone within a border agrees.**

> What matters is *when* you choose. There's a window early on when the
> decision is nearly free — a habit, a line of paint. Then cars get
> built, roads get drawn, and buses get doors, and the window closes.
> After that, the choice isn't yours. It belongs to whoever made it
> first.

> Sweden proved you *can* force it open again. It cost them years of
> planning and a nation standing still in the road at dawn — and they
> only did it because the cost of *not* switching had finally grown
> larger.

> Most things in your life are like this. Not the best option. Just the
> one that got there early enough to become expensive to leave.

### [7:45] — CLOSE

> This is **Plainsight.** One ordinary thing, and the hidden logic
> holding it up.

> Next time: why your keyboard is arranged like that — and why the
> obvious explanation is probably a myth.

**[END — 8:00]**

## Fact-check before publishing

This episode is heavier on history than Episode 01, which means more ways
to be confidently wrong. The script is already hedged in the right places
(the sword-arm origin is explicitly flagged as tradition) — keep those
hedges in the final cut.

- [ ] Left/right population split — verify current share and cite year
- [ ] Number of countries/territories driving on the left
- [ ] Sword-arm origin — confirm it stays framed as tradition, not fact
- [ ] Wagon/postilion explanation for keeping right — find a real source
- [ ] Napoleonic spread of right-hand traffic — verify, avoid overclaiming
- [ ] British formalisation of keeping left — get the actual statute and date
- [ ] Dagen H: 3 September 1967, and the referendum result and year
- [ ] Sweden's crash figures before/after — find the actual data; the
      "dropped then drifted back" claim needs a source, not a vibe
- [ ] Samoa 2009 switch and its stated economic reasoning
- [ ] Myanmar switch date and the RHD-import situation today
- [ ] Confirm no credible study shows one side safer

## Production notes

- **[4:00] is the emotional centre.** An entire country stopping in the
  road is the image people will remember. Slow down and let it play.
- **[5:30]** should feel like a genuine turn — the audience expects
  "and it went badly." Give it a beat of silence first.
- Keep both sides visually neutral all episode. If the animation makes
  one side look correct, the thesis breaks.
- Chapter the upload; the Sweden section will get linked on its own.

## Animation notes

Same visual identity as Episode 01 (`../animation/README.md`). Two shots
carry the episode:

- **The lock-in shot:** one car at centre; around it, small charcoal
  icons appear one at a time — steering wheel, bus door, toll booth,
  motorway exit, intersection. None is dramatic. The *accumulation* is
  the point. Let it get uncomfortably crowded.
- **Dagen H:** top-down view of a road full of cars, all on the left.
  Everything stops. Long hold on total stillness — longer than feels
  comfortable. Then every car moves across together, and motion resumes.
  Single unbroken take, locked-off camera. No music under the stillness.
